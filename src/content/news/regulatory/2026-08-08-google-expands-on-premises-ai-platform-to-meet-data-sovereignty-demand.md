body_en: |
  Google has extended its Google Distributed Cloud platform to run artificial intelligence workloads on-premises, targeting enterprises and governments that face regulatory or security constraints on where data can be processed and stored.

  The move addresses a tension that has slowed AI adoption in regulated sectors: organisations want access to advanced AI models but are often prohibited, or reluctant, to send sensitive data to public cloud infrastructure operated outside their jurisdiction.

  ## What Google Distributed Cloud offers

  Google Distributed Cloud extends Google Cloud services into environments owned and operated by the customer. Google is offering it in two deployment configurations.

  The first is an air-gapped version, fully disconnected from Google Cloud and the public internet. Google states that in this configuration the system cannot be remotely shut down by the company — a commitment aimed directly at customers concerned about dependence on an overseas provider.

  The second is a connected version, in which Google manages the software lifecycle while the system runs on the customer's existing hardware.

  Both configurations provide on-premises infrastructure for AI workloads, access to Gemini models and open-source models, and inference services — the compute layer that runs AI model queries.

  ## Survey findings driving the pitch

  Google cited a survey of more than 1,400 senior IT leaders in support of the announcement. The research found that 48% of respondents were prioritising infrastructure with data residency controls to meet local data security laws. Data residency refers to requirements that data be stored and processed within a specific geographic or legal jurisdiction.

  The same survey found that 52% of organisations now use a hybrid cloud approach to AI — combining on-premises systems with multicloud environments — rather than relying on a single infrastructure type.

  ## Three risks shaping customer decisions

  Google identified three categories of risk it says are influencing how organisations structure their AI infrastructure.

  The first is jurisdictional risk: changing regulation, intellectual property concerns, and the possibility of foreign governments demanding access to data held on overseas infrastructure are pushing organisations to keep data local.

  The second is economic independence. Some customers are concerned that relying on foreign infrastructure providers could expose critical services to disruption or coercion.

  The third is geopolitical risk, particularly for operators of essential services — such as energy, transport, and communications networks — that want to reduce exposure to global instability.

  ## Competitive context

  Google is not alone in pursuing this segment. The source notes that Amazon Web Services and Microsoft have made comparable moves to serve customers in government, defence, healthcare, finance, and critical infrastructure. Those buyers frequently require formal guarantees about where data sits and how systems continue to operate if external connectivity is lost.

  ## What the numbers indicate

  The survey results suggest compliance has moved from a niche concern to a mainstream factor in AI infrastructure planning. With nearly half of senior IT leaders citing data residency as a priority, governance requirements are now shaping architecture decisions alongside cost and performance.

  The finding that a majority of organisations have adopted hybrid approaches indicates that few large organisations view a single-environment strategy — either fully on-premises or fully public cloud — as viable. Instead, they are building mixed architectures that keep sensitive workloads local while drawing on external resources for other tasks.

  Google's argument is that on-premises AI no longer requires accepting isolation from current model development. The platform is positioned as a way for customers to maintain local data control without forgoing access to the AI capabilities available through Google Cloud.

  ## Google擴大地端AI平台，因應資料主權需求

  Google已擴充旗下Google分散式雲端（Google Distributed Cloud）平台，使其能夠在地端環境執行人工智慧工作負載，主要鎖定面臨法規或資安限制、對資料處理與儲存地點有所規範的企業與政府機構。

  此舉正面回應了一個長期拖慢受監管產業AI導入速度的矛盾：各組織希望取用先進AI模型，卻往往受到規定限制或自身顧慮，無法將敏感資料傳送至境外的公有雲基礎設施。

  ## Google分散式雲端的功能

  Google分散式雲端將Google Cloud服務延伸至客戶自行擁有並運營的環境中。Google提供兩種部署配置供客戶選擇。

  第一種是「氣隙隔離」版本，與Google Cloud及公共網際網路完全斷開。Google表示，在此配置下，該公司無法遠端關閉客戶系統——這項承諾直接針對那些擔憂過度依賴境外供應商的客戶。

  第二種是「連線」版本，由Google負責管理軟體生命週期，系統則運行於客戶現有的硬體之上。

  兩種配置均提供地端AI工作負載基礎設施、Gemini模型與開源模型的存取權限，以及推論服務——即負責執行AI模型查詢的運算層。

  ## 驅動此次發表的調查結果

  Google引用一項針對逾1,400名資深IT主管所做的調查，作為此次發表的佐證依據。研究發現，48%的受訪者正在優先建置具備資料落地管控能力的基礎設施，以符合當地資料安全法規。所謂「資料落地」，係指要求資料必須在特定地理或法律管轄範圍內儲存與處理的相關規定。

  同一份調查也發現，52%的組織目前採用混合雲方式推動AI應用——亦即將地端系統與多雲環境結合使用——而非仰賴單一基礎設施類型。

  ## 影響客戶決策的三大風險

  Google點出三類風險，並指出這些因素正在左右各組織建構AI基礎設施的方式。

  第一是「司法管轄風險」：法規的變動、智慧財產權疑慮，以及境外政府要求存取儲存於海外基礎設施之資料的可能性，促使各組織傾向將資料留在本地。

  第二是「經濟自主性」。部分客戶擔憂，過度依賴境外基礎設施供應商，可能導致關鍵服務面臨中斷或脅迫的風險。

  第三是「地緣政治風險」，尤其對於能源、交通及通訊網路等關鍵服務的營運者而言，他們希望降低自身對全球局勢動盪的曝險程度。

  ## 競爭背景

  Google並非唯一搶進這塊市場的業者。報導指出，Amazon Web Services與Microsoft也已採取類似行動，積極服務政府、國防、醫療、金融及關鍵基礎設施等領域的客戶。這類買家往往需要正式保證，確認資料存放位置，以及一旦外部連線中斷後系統仍能持續運作。

  ## 數字透露的訊號

  上述調查結果顯示，合規問題已從小眾考量演變為AI基礎設施規劃的主流因素。近半數資深IT主管將資料落地列為優先事項，意味著治理要求如今已和成本、效能並列，共同左右著架構決策。

  多數組織已採行混合架構的調查結果，亦顯示幾乎沒有大型組織認為「單一環境策略」——無論是全面地端或全面公有雲——是可行的選項。取而代之的，是建構混合架構，將敏感工作負載保留在本地，同時在其他任務上借助外部資源。

  Google的論點是：採用地端AI不再意味著必須與最新模型發展脫節。這套平台定位為讓客戶在維持本地資料掌控權的同時，不必放棄透過Google Cloud取用AI能力的選項。
