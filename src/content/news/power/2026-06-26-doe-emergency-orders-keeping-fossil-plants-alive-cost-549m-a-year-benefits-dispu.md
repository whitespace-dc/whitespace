body_en: |
  The U.S. Department of Energy's ongoing campaign of emergency orders preventing fossil-fueled generating units from retiring is costing ratepayers roughly $1.5 million a day — approximately $548.8 million a year — while four of the 11 units ordered to stay online are not currently operating, according to industry data and analysts, raising questions about whether the policy is delivering the grid reliability benefits DOE cites as its rationale.

  DOE has issued the orders under Section 202(c) of the Federal Power Act, which authorizes the department to direct generating units to continue operating during emergency conditions affecting power supply. Since 2025, it has applied that authority to units at seven power plants, citing potential future supply shortfalls, growing electricity demand from data centers, and the performance of some units during a severe January winter storm.

  ## Costs flowing to ratepayers

  The Sierra Club, in a June 8 report, calculated net expenses for the seven plants at approximately $1.5 million a day, or $548.8 million annually. The Institute for Energy Economics and Financial Analysis (IEEFA), a nonprofit that advocates moving away from fossil fuels, put the total at more than $30 million a month, or more than $300 million through mid-May. Costs could increase further if plants under orders require repairs, IEEFA noted.

  The bill is already reaching utility customers. Consumers Energy, the majority owner of the J.H. Campbell power plant in West Olive, Michigan, reported spending $401 million to keep the plant online through March 31, according to an April 28 filing with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. The company recovered $221 million through power sales in the Midcontinent Independent System Operator (MISO) market — MISO is the grid operator covering a large swath of the central United States — but plans to seek $180 million in cost recovery from ratepayers in MISO's northern and central regions. DOE issued its latest 90-day order for the Campbell plant on May 18, extending its required operation through Aug. 16.

  TransAlta, an independent power producer based in Calgary, Canada, is seeking $19.9 million from ratepayers to cover costs for keeping its Centralia power plant from retiring, according to an April 30 filing with the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC), the federal agency overseeing interstate electricity markets. TransAlta estimates it would need to spend an additional $23 million to repair the plant if DOE issues further orders. The company is preparing to convert the coal-fired Centralia plant to run on natural gas in a deal with Puget Sound Energy.

  ## DOE's reliability rationale

  DOE Associate Deputy Secretary of Energy Alex Fitzsimmons defended the orders in a recent interview. "What we know from what NERC has told us is that resource adequacy is worsening all across the country, and had been for several years due to the premature retirement of reliable dispatchable generation at the same time that energy demand is rising," Fitzsimmons said, referring to the North American Electric Reliability Corporation, the nonprofit body responsible for bulk power system reliability standards in the U.S. Dispatchable generation refers to power sources that can be ramped up on demand, as opposed to wind or solar output that varies with weather. "We need to make sure that we have dispatchable generation to meet peak demand," Fitzsimmons said.

  DOE has pointed to Winter Storm Fern — a late-January cold weather event that drove elevated electricity demand across large parts of the country — as evidence that some of the plants under orders delivered real value. During that storm, two units at the R.M. Schahfer generating station in Indiana, owned by Northern Indiana Public Service Co. (NIPSCO), operated at over 285 megawatts each day, DOE said in its June 18 order extending the Schahfer units' required operation.

  In ordering the Orlando Utilities Commission (OUC), a Florida municipal utility, to keep operating its nearly 465-megawatt coal-fired Stanton Unit 1 in early June rather than placing it in "cold shutdown," DOE acknowledged that NERC's most recent long-term reliability assessment rated Florida at "normal risk" for long-term energy adequacy. DOE nonetheless cited NERC's finding that projections for resource and transmission growth in Florida lag what is needed to support new data centers and other large electricity loads.

  DOE makes its 202(c) decisions based on the facts and circumstances in each case, Fitzsimmons said.

  ## Grid experts question the emergency designation

  Independent analysts and grid operators are disputing DOE's framing. Nikhil Kumar, a program director at GridLab, a nonprofit technical consulting organization focused on the electric grid, said NERC's recent summer and winter reliability assessments indicate minimal near-term risk in the regions the orders affect.

  "There is no emergency," Kumar said.

  NERC's summer reliability assessment, released in May, rated MISO — the grid operator whose territory includes the Schahfer plant and the Culley Unit 2 plant in Indiana — as facing "normal" reliability risks. When CenterPoint Energy sought permission to retire its Culley Unit 2 on Dec. 31 as planned, Michael Roeder, CenterPoint Energy's Indiana region president, said the unit is not needed to support grid reliability, citing NERC assessments and reports from MISO and state utility regulators.

  DOE cited tight capacity auction results in MISO as one rationale for keeping the Campbell, Schahfer, and Culley units online. But MISO's most recent capacity auction, held this spring, cleared with a reserve margin 3.5 percentage points above MISO's 7.9% reliability reserve target, meaning the grid carried a cushion above the level the operator requires for safe and reliable operation. An annual MISO survey released June 3 found that anticipated capacity additions over the next five years will outpace rising electricity demand.

  Grid operators and state utility regulators routinely review power plant retirement decisions to verify that units can be taken offline without causing reliability problems, Kumar noted. He acknowledged that adding capacity to the grid always provides some incremental reliability benefit, but said NERC data do not support the "emergency" label.

  NERC itself offered a more nuanced assessment. "It's a blunt instrument used as a last resort to keep necessary generation online and has certainly helped, especially in this past winter, maintain reliability," John Moura, NERC's director of reliability assessments and performance analysis, said at a FERC meeting in February. "Some are helpful, some maybe not. But in the aggregate, it's definitely been a help to keep units online."

  ## Short-term orders, long-term planning gaps

  Beyond cost, the 90-day duration of individual orders creates complications for grid and utility planning. Erin Melly, a vice president at research firm Capstone, said the short order cycles do not align with the multi-year planning horizon used by grid operators and utilities. "From a planning perspective, this timeline doesn't necessarily align with how a grid operator plans years in advance," she said.

  The 90-day structure also generates uncertainty for plant owners weighing repair and maintenance decisions. TransAlta's estimate of $23 million in additional repair costs if further orders are issued illustrates how the open-ended nature of the program can complicate financial planning for affected companies.

  Capstone expects DOE to issue additional 90-day orders to delay further coal retirements, with additional gigawatts of capacity and coal units scheduled to retire before 2028, Melly said.

  Industry observers also note that the plants under the orders may be displacing cheaper power supplies and contributing to air pollution, adding costs beyond those captured in utility filings, though the sources do not quantify those effects.

  The Campbell plant's current 90-day order runs through Aug. 16, with DOE's next decision on its status required before that date.
body_zh: |
  美國能源部（DOE）持續援引《聯邦電力法》（Federal Power Act）第202(c)條，發出緊急命令阻止化石燃料發電機組退役，每天讓用電戶承擔約150萬美元的費用，一年累計高達5億4,880萬美元。然而在被迫繼續運轉的11組機組中，有4組目前根本未在運轉。業界數據與分析師的調查結果令各界開始質疑，這項政策究竟能否真正發揮能源部所宣稱的電網可靠性效益。

  能源部自2025年起，援引第202(c)條款對七座電廠旗下機組相繼發出命令，理由包括未來潛在的電力短缺、資料中心帶動的用電需求攀升，以及部分機組在今年一月嚴峻冬季風暴期間的運轉表現。

  ## 費用由用電戶買單

  環保組織山巒協會（Sierra Club）在6月8日發布的報告中，估算七座電廠的淨費用約為每天150萬美元，換算年度費用為5億4,880萬美元。致力推動棄用化石燃料的非營利機構「能源經濟與財務分析研究所」（IEEFA）則估計每月逾3,000萬美元，截至五月中旬累計已超過3億美元。IEEFA並指出，若被命令維持運轉的電廠需要設備維修，費用還可能進一步增加。

  帳單已開始落到電力用戶頭上。消費者能源公司（Consumers Energy）擁有密西根州西奧利夫市J.H.坎貝爾電廠（J.H. Campbell）的多數股權，根據該公司4月28日向美國證券交易委員會（SEC）提交的申報文件，截至3月31日已為維持該廠運轉支出4億100萬美元。公司透過向中部大陸獨立系統運營商（MISO，覆蓋美國中部大片地區的電網調度機構）市場售電，收回2億2,100萬美元，但仍計畫向MISO北部與中部地區的用電戶申請1億8,000萬美元的費用回收。能源部於5月18日再度對坎貝爾電廠發出最新一份90天命令，將強制運轉期限延長至8月16日。

  總部位於加拿大卡加利的獨立發電業者TransAlta，根據4月30日向聯邦能源管理委員會（FERC，負責監管跨州電力市場的聯邦機構）提交的申報文件，正就旗下中央電廠（Centralia）暫緩退役的相關費用，向用電戶申請1,990萬美元補償。TransAlta估計，若能源部再發進一步命令，還需另外投入2,300萬美元進行設備維修。該公司正規劃與普吉特灣能源公司（Puget Sound Energy）合作，將這座燃煤中央電廠改建為天然氣發電廠。

  ## 能源部援引電網可靠性理由

  能源部副次長助理亞歷克斯·菲茨西蒙斯（Alex Fitzsimmons）近日接受採訪時，為上述命令進行辯護。他引述北美電力可靠性委員會（NERC，負責美國大規模電力系統可靠性標準的非營利機構）的說法表示：「NERC告訴我們，全美各地的電力資源充裕性持續惡化，且已惡化多年，根源在於可靠的可調度發電容量提前退役，同時用電需求又在持續上升。」所謂可調度發電，係指能依需求隨時調節出力的電源，有別於輸出量隨天候變化的風力或太陽能。菲茨西蒙斯表示：「我們必須確保有足夠的可調度發電能力，以應對尖峰需求。」

  能源部並以「蕨」冬季風暴（Winter Storm Fern）——一月下旬造成全美大範圍用電需求驟增的寒流事件——為證據，指稱部分受命運轉的電廠確實發揮了實際效益。能源部在6月18日延長印第安納州R.M.沙哈弗發電站（R.M. Schahfer，由北印第安納公共服務公司NIPSCO所有）機組強制運轉期的命令中指出，該廠兩組機組在那場風暴期間每日均維持超過285百萬瓦（MW）的發電量。

  在責令奧蘭多公用事業委員會（OUC，一家佛羅里達州市立電力公司）暫緩將近465百萬瓦的燃煤史坦頓一號機組（Stanton Unit 1）轉入「冷備用」狀態的命令中，能源部坦承，NERC最新發布的長期可靠性評估已將佛羅里達州的長期能源充裕度評定為「正常風險」等級。儘管如此，能源部仍援引NERC的另一項發現：佛羅里達州的電源與輸電擴建預測進度，落後於支撐新建資料中心及其他大型用電負載所需的水準。

  菲茨西蒙斯表示，能源部就每項202(c)決定，均依各案的事實與具體情況個別判斷。

  ## 電網專家質疑緊急認定

  獨立分析師與電網運營商對能源部的論述提出異議。專注電網議題的非營利技術顧問機構GridLab計畫主任尼基爾·庫馬爾（Nikhil Kumar）指出，NERC最近發布的夏季與冬季可靠性評估，顯示受命令波及地區幾乎不存在短期風險。

  「根本沒有緊急情況，」庫馬爾說。

  NERC於五月公布的夏季可靠性評估，將中部大陸獨立系統運營商（MISO，管轄範圍涵蓋沙哈弗電廠及印第安納州卡利二號機組Culley Unit 2）評定為「正常」可靠性風險。CenterPoint Energy申請按計畫於12月31日讓卡利二號機組退役時，該公司印第安納地區總裁麥可·羅德（Michael Roeder）援引NERC評估報告及MISO與州公用事業監管機構的報告指出，該機組並非維繫電網可靠性所必需。

  能源部以MISO容量拍賣結果偏緊為理由之一，要求坎貝爾、沙哈弗及卡利等機組繼續運轉。但MISO今春舉行的最新一輪容量拍賣，結算時的備用容量裕度比MISO設定的7.9%可靠性備用目標高出3.5個百分點，意即電網實際承載的緩衝量已超過運營商確保安全穩定運轉所要求的水準。MISO於6月3日發布的年度調查報告亦顯示，未來五年預計新增的發電容量將超越持續攀升的用電需求。

  庫馬爾指出，電網運營商與州公用事業監管機構例行審查電廠退役申請，以確認機組離線不會造成可靠性問題。他承認增加電網容量始終能提供一定程度的可靠性效益，但強調NERC的數據並不支持「緊急」的定性。

  NERC本身則提出較為細緻的評估。NERC可靠性評估與績效分析主任約翰·穆拉（John Moura）在今年2月的聯邦能源管理委員會（FERC）會議上表示：「這是一個生硬的工具，作為最後手段用來讓必要的發電機組繼續運轉，肯定是有幫助的，尤其在去年這個冬天，確實有助於維護可靠性。有些命令有效，有些未必。但整體而言，讓這些機組持續上線肯定是有幫助的。」

  ## 短期命令，長期規劃之困

  除費用問題之外，單次90天的命令期限也為電網與電力公司的規劃帶來複雜性。研究機構Capstone副總裁艾琳·梅利（Erin Melly）表示，短暫的命令週期與電網運營商及電力公司動輒多年的規劃時程並不吻合。她說：「從規劃角度來看，這個時間框架未必能與電網運營商提前數年進行規劃的方式相互配合。」

  90天的結構也讓電廠業主在權衡設備維修與保養決策時面臨不確定性。TransAlta估計若再收到進一步命令將需額外承擔2,300萬美元維修費用，正說明這項開放式政策如何讓受波及企業的財務規劃更加複雜。

  Capstone預期能源部將繼續發出90天命令，進一步延緩更多燃煤電廠退役。梅利指出，2028年以前還有更多十億瓦（GW）級的發電容量與燃煤機組預計退役。

  業界觀察人士也注意到，受命令維持運轉的電廠可能正在排擠更廉價的電力來源，並加劇空氣污染，在電力公司申報文件所呈現的成本之外造成額外負擔，但相關資料來源未對這些影響進行量化。

  坎貝爾電廠目前的90天命令效期至8月16日，能源部須在該日期之前，就其後續狀態作出決定。
